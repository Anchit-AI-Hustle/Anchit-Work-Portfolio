Independent product case study · November 2026

What happens after a Lenskart eye test? A product case study, with a working prototype.

An outside-in look at Lenskart, the Indian eyewear retailer, built only from public data: its filings and earnings calls, its website and apps, and 3,500 public app reviews. I cannot see Lenskart's internal data, and Lenskart may well have tested some of this already. It is my own work, not Lenskart's.

Read the 1-minute summary Try the prototype See the evidence

The findingLenskart ran 2.07 crore (20.7 million) eye tests in India in FY26, and 70 lakh more in the last quarter alone. From the outside, I could not find anything that tells those customers when their next one is due.

The idea to testA Vision Recall Engine: follow up people after their first eye test, tell each customer when their next one is due, and suggest lenses or a new pair only after a test says one is needed. Aimed first at first-time testers and children.

Slide 01 · In 60 seconds

The short version

1 · The problem

  • A free eye test leaves a dated prescription on file.
  • From public data, nothing tells the customer when to come back.
  • A first-timer whose test found a power, but who did not buy, gets no follow-up I could find.
  • This is about prescription updates, not style.

2 · Who it is for

  • First-time testers: "more than half" of Lenskart's 70 lakh tests last quarter.
  • Children, whose eyes change every year; reminders go to the parent.
  • Not routine reminders for adults: most adults' prescriptions barely move until 40, so adults get one only if they ask.

3 · What exists, what I would add

Live today

  • Free and ₹99 home eye tests, saved prescriptions
  • Free replacement lenses with a ₹199 fitting fee, Gold membership (1 crore members)
  • Store booking, and calls to confirm lens power

I would add

  • A follow-up for first-timers who did not buy
  • A due date for each customer's next test
  • A reminder for children and for adults who ask

4 · The numbers, with caveats

  • The follow-up pays for its messages if 1 in about 470 people messaged orders who otherwise would not.
  • My scenario: about ₹3.3 cr a year of contribution on first orders, in a range from ₹0.2 cr to ₹6.3 cr. Every lift in it is unproven.
  • Lenskart may already have tried this. Only a test against a holdout would tell.
Slide 02 · The problem

People Lenskart has already tested get no prompt to come back for their eyes

What I am trying to solve

  • For Lenskart, not for the market. Bring back customers Lenskart has already tested, when their eyes have probably changed. More eye tests across India would be a side effect, not the goal.
  • Two moments. A first-time tester whose test found a power but who did not buy. And a child, whose power changes every year, when the next test falls due.
  • Prescription, not style. Style already has its triggers: new collections, Gold's second pair, and the examples of a trigger Lenskart's CEO gives are collections. The prescription date is the clock I could not see anyone using.

Fair questions, and my honest answers

The eye test is free. If nothing changed, will they buy?
Mostly not, and that is the weak point. I assume 40% of re-tests find no change and sell nothing; those customers are told to keep their pair. That is why most of the value sits with first-timers, not with reminders.
Won't customers come back anyway?
Many will. Customers Lenskart acquired in FY23 bought 3.6 eyeglasses within two years. The idea only matters for the ones who do not, and public data does not say how many that is.
Customers only open the app when they need glasses.
Agreed, so the app cannot be the channel. The first touch is WhatsApp, and the one that worked best in published trials is a phone call. Lenskart has called customers to confirm lens power since at least 2017 (slide 06).
Slide 03 · How often

Eyes change fastest in childhood; most adults' prescriptions barely move until 40

How often people should get tested

  • UK College of Optometrists: without a clinical reason, do not recall adults more often than every two years.
  • Common US guidance: yearly for children, every two years for most adults, yearly from 65.
  • I found no official interval in India.

How often they actually do

  • Rural Tamil Nadu: 15.5% of adults had ever had an eye examination (TREE study, 2019 to 2020).
  • United States: 17 months between exams on average (The Vision Council, 2023).
  • I found no national figure for India.

How often people buy glasses

  • Lenskart's FY23 customers: 3.6 eyeglasses within two years (Lenskart, disclosed).
  • The India average: 1.8 eyeglasses in two years (Lenskart's FY26 annual report, beside its own 3.6).

What this means. A prescription does not change every year for most adults, so a yearly or two-yearly reminder to every adult would mostly find no change. Lenskart's own customers already buy 3.6 eyeglasses within two years, far more often than adult prescriptions change. The prescription clock matters most for children and for people tested for the first time.

Five possible gaps, as far as public evidence shows

App ratings, read on 14 Nov 2026

Google Play shows its star split as percentages (1★ 6.5%, 2★ 1.1%); the App Store gives counts (8,714 one-star and 989 two-star). Google Play lists 5 crore+ downloads.

What that suggests

  • About 9 in 10 people who rate the app give it 3 stars or more, so a reminder would mostly reach satisfied customers.
  • Under 2% of Google Play installs leave a rating at all. Most people open the app only when they need glasses, so the app cannot carry a reminder on its own.
  • The low ratings are mostly about delivery, support and refunds, but the one that matters here is a wrong power: a reminder that leads to a bad re-test does harm.

In customers' own words

I read 3,500 public reviews of the Lenskart app: the 3,000 most recent on Google Play (28 Aug to 12 Nov 2026) and the 500 most recent on the App Store (29 Apr to 11 Nov 2026), collected 13 Nov 2026. On Google Play 1,946 are 4-5★, mostly short praise. Of the 1,458 rated 1-3★ across both stores, by keyword match 243 mention delivery, 235 customer support, 220 refunds, returns or exchanges and 148 power, lens or product quality (33 of them a wrong or different power); a review can match more than one. Keyword counts are a blunt tool, and written reviews skew negative against a 4.5 rating. Quoted verbatim; names left out.

What this says: the eye test is what customers trust or distrust most, so a recall has to protect the power reading before it sells anything; returning customers notice when a new customer gets a better deal; and nobody in 3,500 reviews mentions being told their next test was due.

ShortcomingEvidenceSo what
1. The second eye test has no owner2.07 crore India tests in FY26, each leaving a dated, saved prescription. No reminder, recall or prescription-age message is public. The CEO's examples of a trigger are collections, such as a wedding range that "ran out of stock very fast".The only trigger a customer gets is fashion; the clinical one is unused.
2. Churn is acknowledged, not measured in public"Consumers do churn." The disclosed "2-year repeat rate ... about 98%" is repeat orders divided by first orders, not the share of customers who return. No retention or churn rate is disclosed.Heavy buyers can hide the people who never come back.
3. First-timers buy slowly, and nobody follows upIndia eye tests +54.3% in FY26 against customer accounts +24.2%. Management says a significant majority of tested people buy and first-timers take longer, and names the fix: "better CRM to bring these people into the fold" (Q1 FY27 call).The gap is deliberate. The open question is who follows up with the slow ones.
4. Gold's clock is the renewal date, not the prescription dateGold "was designed many years back to address" churn. It is "a second pair, lens upgrades, exclusive offers", and management plans to add "eye-care benefits and services on the app". 83 reviews mention Gold or membership; 71 of them are 1★.The fee already covers a free home eye test; nothing I found tells a member when to use it.
5. Trust in the power reading is fragile33 low-rated reviews mention a wrong or different power or lens, and 148 mention power, lens or product quality more broadly. One describes three tests in two weeks with a different power each time.A recall that leads to a wrong pair does more harm than no recall.
Slide 06 · Counter-evidence

Why Lenskart probably isn't running a recall, and is probably right for most customers

My read. Lenskart uses the eye test to win new customers. A routine re-test of a settled adult usually finds nothing to sell, and it takes an optometrist slot a new customer could have had. On public evidence, leaving it out looks like a deliberate and sensible choice, not an oversight.

ReasonPublic evidenceWhat I changed because of it
1. The eye test is for acquisition"Our mandate is to accelerate new customer acquisition" and "Every eye test expands the addressable market itself" (Q4 FY26 shareholders' letter). 2.38 crore tests in FY26, about half of them first-time in India.Lead with the First Prescription Follow-up. Following up a first-timer is acquisition, and it needs no second test.
2. Most adults' eyes don't changeRefraction stays stable from early adulthood to about 50 (Tehran Eye Study). Children's myopia grows by about 0.5 dioptre a year, and stops in half of them by 15 and three quarters by 18 (US National Academies, 2024).No routine reminder for adults unless they ask for one at the test. Children's reminders go to the parent.
3. Optometrist time is the bottleneckLenskart calls the optometrist one of "the scarcest skills in eyewear", and India eye tests grew 44.1% last quarter.Re-tests are booked into open slots only, never a first-timer's.
4. A prescription trigger sells lenses; style sells framesThe CEO says customers lack "triggers to buy more eyewear" and gives collections as the example. Replacement lenses are free with a ₹199 fitting fee, against a ₹1,950 selling price, and India selling price fell from about ₹1,760 to ₹1,609 when that offer launched.The lens-swap loss is a line in my sizing. On its own the adult reminder loses money, so it is the last thing to test.
5. Customers already come backCustomers acquired in FY23 bought 3.6 eyeglasses within two years.Expect a small lift, and read it only against a holdout.
6. Call rules tightened in 2025Since TRAI's February 2025 amendment, promotional calls must come from 140-series numbers and service calls from 1600-series; ordinary 10-digit numbers cannot be used. The DPDP Act needs clear, withdrawable consent.Calls only from Lenskart's registered service line, only to people who agreed at the test. No store-phone calls.
7. The capability already existsLenskart has called customers to confirm their lens power since at least 2017, from one registered number (Lenskart and Truecaller, May 2017). Stores have their own numbers behind an IVR.So the absence looks like a choice. My first question would be whether a reminder call was ever tested, and what it showed.

Recall is standard practice elsewhere

The UK College of Optometrists tells its members not to recall adults more often than every two years without a clinical reason. India has no such norm, so Lenskart would be setting it.

Reminders work, by very different amounts

In US eye clinics, an automated call with a letter lifted follow-up from 28% to 30% and a personal call with a letter to 38% (Callinan et al., 2017); a brochure, a personal letter and a call lifted completed exams from 30% to 48% (Zangalli et al., 2016). In China, two informational text messages before a booked diabetic eye visit lifted attendance from 14.0% to 42.9% (Chen et al., 2018). All are clinics, not shops.

A rival already does it

Titan's Encircle programme: "You can opt for a reminder for an eye test and contact lens refill every 6 months". Titan Eye+ recommends "an eye-test every six months". Lenskart would be catching up, with a reminder set by the customer's age and power instead of one rhythm for everyone.

Slide 08 · The idea to test

The Vision Recall Engine: six features to test, on top of what Lenskart already runs

Narrowed by slide 06. It reads the eye-test record Lenskart already holds, follows up people whose first test found a power, reminds parents of children and adults who ask, and suggests lenses or a pair only after a test says one is needed. Each link opens that feature in the prototype.

Feature 1Recall Date

When each customer's next eye test is due, from the date of their last test and their age band. Shown to everyone in the app; a message goes only to parents of children and to adults who asked.

For: the CRM teamSee the Recall Date
Feature 2Recall Reminder

One short WhatsApp message when a test falls due: to the parent for under-18s, and to adults only if they asked at their test. A slot to book, no price or discount.

For: the wearer or the parentOpen the Recall Reminder
Feature 3Recall Booking

The eye test booked in two taps into an open optometrist slot, never a first-timer's. If an overdue customer has not booked by day 8, one call from Lenskart's registered service line, only if they agreed.

For: the customer and the storeSee Neha's booking
Feature 4Recall Outcome

What follows the test, set by its result: keep your pair if the power is unchanged, new lenses in your current frame if it changed, a referral if glasses are not the answer.

For: the customerSee the outcome
Feature 5First Prescription Follow-up

Two messages in 30 days to someone whose first eye test found a power and who has not ordered: their saved power and two ways to try frames. Then it stops.

For: first-time customersSee Ravi's follow-up
Feature 6Recall Dashboard

The CRM team's weekly screen: each group, the action it gets, its holdout, and results against the holdout once a pilot runs.

For: the CRM teamOpen the dashboard

What pays first. On my numbers the First Prescription Follow-up earns all of it and more: about ₹3.8 cr, against ₹3.3 cr net overall. The call in Recall Booking adds about ₹0.5 cr. The Recall Reminder on its own loses about ₹0.3 cr, because offering the cheaper lens swap first costs some returners a new pair. Build the follow-up first; test the reminder last, and drop it if it does not earn its place. See the sizing

Reuses (already live)

  • The saved prescription and its date, from 2.07 crore India tests a year
  • Store appointment booking and the ₹99 home eye test
  • Free Lens Replacement and the lens packages
  • Lenskart Gold and the face-scan profile
  • The in-house CRM, and the registered number Lenskart already uses to call customers about their lens power

Adds (not found today)

  • A due date for every customer's next eye test, shown in the app
  • A reminder for children's parents and adults who ask, with no price
  • One consented call from the service line when an overdue customer has not booked
  • Booking into open optometrist slots only
  • A next step that depends on the re-test result
  • A follow-up for first-time tests with no order
  • A holdout to prove incremental lift

Where the AI is

  • The due date is a rule, on purpose: it has to be explainable to a customer and owned by optometry.
  • A model ranks who is most likely to act, to order the call list.
  • Language models draft each reminder in the customer's language and answer questions, inside fixed rules: no price, no diagnosis.

What I would not build

  • No discount in any recall message.
  • No AI-read power; the optometrist decides.
  • No message to a child; it goes to the parent.
  • No contact-lens reorder or trade-in in the first release.
  • No routine reminder to adults who did not ask.
  • No calls from store phones; one call, from the service line.

Who owns it

  • CRM owns the engine and the holdout.
  • Optometry owns the intervals and the symptom rules.
  • Customer service owns the day-8 call list.
  • Legal owns consent and the children's flow.

Intervals follow published guidance: the American Optometric Association (yearly from 6 to 17, at least every two years from 18 to 64, yearly from 65), the UK College of Optometrists (no routine recall more often than every two years from 16) and the International Myopia Institute (six-monthly follow-up for children on myopia control). Lenskart's optometry team would set the final ones. India has no legal prescription expiry, so the due date would be Lenskart's own clinical claim.

Slide 11 · A 90-day test

If it is worth testing: prove lift in two groups first

Weeks 1-3 · Baseline
  • Ask first: has Lenskart tested a reminder or a follow-up before, and what did it show?
  • Measure how many tested first-timers leave without ordering, and when they come back
  • Backtest: does prescription age predict the next order?
  • Legal review of consent, and the service-line set-up for calls
Weeks 4-8 · Pilot
  • First Prescription Follow-up in Tier 2+ stores (smaller cities), 50/50 by customer
  • A "remind me" question at the eye test, to measure how many adults and parents say yes
  • Re-tests only in open slots, capped at 5% of a store's weekly tests
Weeks 9-13 · Next step
  • Children's reminders to parents, after legal sign-off
  • Adult reminders and the call, last, and only if the follow-up works
  • First read-out at 60 days; the extra-or-earlier question at 180
  • Scale decision; 90/10 holdout at scale
North star

Incremental revenue per due customer against the holdout at 60 days.

Leading

Delivered rate, reminder to booking, show-up rate, re-tests within 30 days of the due date, lens-swap share.

Guardrails

WhatsApp opt-outs and blocks, NPS (Lenskart's customer satisfaction score), wrong-power tickets, return rate, optometrist slots used.

Sample size

To detect a 1 point lift on an assumed 6% 60-day order rate, at 80% power and 5% significance, an arm needs about 9,500 customers; that fits the follow-up. The reminder alone implies about half a point, which needs about 37,000 in each arm. Both are sized on order rate; the revenue metric and the store-randomised arm need more, set from the baseline variance measured in weeks 1 to 3.

Data it needs

Test date and result, age band, order history, consent flags, ticket status. Events: reminder sent, delivered, booked, attended, result, order.
